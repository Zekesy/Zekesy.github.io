I have not watched a ton of black and white films, but every single one I have checked out so far has been a hit. The Apartment is a fascinating movie. It is marketed as a romantic comedy, but underneath the wit is a surprisingly dark, cynical look at corporate culture and relationships that fortunately lands on a genuinely heartwarming note.

## Is It Really Just a Comedy?

While the movie is classified as a rom com, there is a heavy, dark undertone running through the whole story that I personally could not ignore. Corporate ladder climbing and marital infidelity form the backbone of the premise. C.C. Baxter lets his married bosses use his apartment for their secret affairs in exchange for favorable performance reviews and promotions. On the surface, the early situations lead to funny, chaotic misunderstandings as Baxter juggles their schedules, but the reality behind those jokes is pretty grim.

The mood shifts significantly once we spend more time with Miss Kubelik, the elevator operator Baxter carries a massive crush on, who turns out to be trapped in an affair with Baxter’s ultimate boss, Mr. Sheldrake.

The movie offers a sharp look at how women were treated in mid century corporate America. They were essentially viewed as disposable objects by these powerful men, who repeatedly lied to them and led them on with fake promises of divorcing their real wives just to get what they wanted. You see the immense mental toll this takes on Miss Kubelik, culminating in her trying to end her life in Baxter's apartment.

Even during that crisis, the film retains its comic edge, like the frantic scene where Baxter and his neighbor march a dazed Miss Kubelik around the room while forcing coffee down her throat to keep her awake. I found myself laughing at the sheer absurdity of the chaos, yet the heavy reality of the situation stayed with me. The entitlement of these corporate executives, who cheat without a shred of remorse and pull every excuse out of a hat to avoid feeling like bad guys, was genuinely wild to watch.

## Nice Guys Do Not Finish Last

I loved the resolution. Even though we only get a quick moment of Mr. Sheldrake sitting completely alone at the Chinese restaurant on New Year’s Eve, it was endlessly satisfying to see him finally get what he deserved.

I am so glad the story ended on an uplifting note. Baxter finally finds his backbone, stops selling out his dignity for a career bump, and chooses his own morality over a fancy office key. That courage inspires Miss Kubelik to find her own self respect, walk away from Sheldrake, and run back to Baxter.

So many modern romances use cheating as a cheap, spicy plot device to create artificial drama, like in The Notebook or Titanic where the taboo nature of the affair is supposed to make it romantic. I love that an older film from 1960 took a clear, grounded stance against that behavior, rewarding Baxter for actually being a decent human being who cares about her well being.

## Timeless, Memorable Dialogue

The dialogue in this movie is packed with witty, memorable lines. The script relies on clever callbacks and natural banter rather than forced drama. After diving deeper into the romance genre lately, I have realized that scriptwriting and natural onscreen chemistry make or break a love story for me. You do not need grand, life or death coincidences when the characters talk like real people, which is the same reason I fell in love with When Harry Met Sally.

Great dialogue grounds the relationship and makes you care. That said, some masterpieces build that same connection visually, like In the Mood for Love or La La Land, using color palettes, music, and quiet atmosphere to create tension without saying a word. But when a movie gets both the tone and the spoken words right, it is magic.

## Final Thoughts

I thoroughly enjoyed The Apartment. Despite its dark satirical edge and heavy themes, it handles its story with so much heart, grace, and humor. It is a brilliant piece of cinema that I would gladly rewatch anytime, and for movies its easily up there for me, romance-wise.
