I went into this show completely blind. All I knew was that it opened with every parent's absolute worst nightmare—Marissa (Sarah Snook) showing up to pick up her son Milo from a playdate, only to find out the address was a fake and he’d been kidnapped.

The first episode instantly sets it up as a massive, high-stakes puzzle. Naturally, I put on my metaphorical Sherlock Holmes top hat, grabbed my magnifying glass, and prepared to outsmart the writers by guessing the culprit immediately.

I could not have been more wrong.

## Twists on Twists (and Overanalyzing Everything)

The entire joy of *All Her Fault* is the desperate, frantic game of trying to guess who did it. To be completely honest, I was changing my prime suspect after almost every single episode.

The writers are absolute masters of intentional misdirection. They drape every single character in shadows, making everyone look just guilty enough to keep you spinning. I found myself heavily falling for every single red herring, overanalyzing minor glances, suspicious text messages, and background details in every scene. Even though my theories were completely off-base, the absolute fun of trying to piece the mystery together made this one of the most engaging bumbles of my detective career.

---

# The Ending: Total Shock Value That Actually Makes Sense

When the final cards are dealt, the ending hits you like a freight train. It is a definite shock, yet when you look back at the crumbs left throughout the episodes, it somehow makes perfect, twisted sense.

Learning the truth about Marissa's "Ken-doll" husband, Peter (Jake Lacy)—the secret baby-switching accident from years ago, the murder of the biological grandfather, and his terrifying web of emotional abuse—completely recontextualizes the whole show. But the real kicker is how Marissa delivers her own version of justice. Using Peter's severe soy allergy against him at a family wake and withholding his EpiPen? Cold. Calculated. Absolutely brilliant.

# Social Commentary: The Suburbia Guilt Trip

Beyond the intense thriller aspects, the core genius of *All Her Fault* is how it highlights our immediate cultural reflex to point fingers—and specifically, how society inherently blames women first for any family tragedy.

The second Milo goes missing, the school gates turn into a toxic breeding ground for gossip. Led by insufferable characters like Sarah, the "parent rep," the community instantly wraps Marissa in a suffocating layer of judgment. Everyone is ready to dissect her parenting, criticize her career, and call her a "terrible mother" for not cross-referencing a text message under duress.

The title *All Her Fault* is a brilliant, sarcastic indictment of this exact double standard. Notice how *nobody* points a finger at Marissa's husband, Peter, or Jenny's husband, Richie, during the initial fallout? The show holds up a mirror to how deeply ingrained this misogyny is; the fathers are treated as clueless bystanders, while the mothers are treated as criminally negligent.

# Weaponized Incompetence and Toxic Modern Marriages

This leads into what I found to be the most frustratingly realistic part of the show: the toxic parenting dynamics and the brilliant depiction of weaponized incompetence.

Both Marissa and Jenny are high-powered, working women carrying the invisible mental load of running their households. The husbands, meanwhile, are absolute masterclasses in toxic behavior. We get enraged watching flashbacks where Peter smiles and says, *"Just tell me what you want me to do,"* completely shifting the mental labor of raising his own child onto Marissa. Then you have Richie, who literally complains about having to watch his own kid for a few hours because he "needs time for himself," or who makes a massive mess in the kitchen and just walks away expecting his wife to clean it.

When the police interviews happen, the men literally stare blankly at the detectives, completely unable to answer basic questions about their own children's daily schedules, looking to their wives to bail them out.

Seeing the sheer emotional exhaustion of these women actually makes the eventual bond between Marissa and Jenny incredibly moving. They recognize that they are both consumed by a unique, heavy guilt that their husbands don't even have the capacity to understand. It really forces the audience to reflect on how heavily we stack the deck against working mothers, and how quickly a community's facade of friendship shatters when fear enters the room.

# Conclusion

Ultimately, *All Her Fault* was an incredible, breakneck ride from the very first minute to the final frame. The pacing was absolutely immaculate, and the constant barrage of twists kept me completely hooked without ever feeling cheap or unearned.

What makes the show so satisfying isn't just the sheer entertainment value, but how meticulously every single character line and underlying mystery is wrapped up by the end. The writers didn't leave us with frustrating, open-ended loose threads; instead, they delivered a completely tight, cohesive conclusion where every seemingly minor detail from the earlier episodes mattered.
